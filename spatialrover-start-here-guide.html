SpatialRover: start here

SpatialRover is the embedded acquisition application. It runs without a desktop UI, talks to physical sensors, establishes time, records synchronized data, reports status, and shuts hardware down safely.

Your boundary: concentrate on everything from a physical sensor through the embedded computer, storage, and network interface. SpatialExplorer is a client and debugging aid, not the center of this path.

Application

rover/rover.cpp owns startup, wiring, state, signal handling, recording managers, and coordinated shutdown.

Navigation

nav.* selects a receiver, parses GNSS/IMU data, determines fine time, and detects time/data problems.

Acquisition

sensorcontroller* configures sensors. sensorrecorder* and managers capture and write data.

Board control

microcontroller.* handles voltage, triggers, odometer and IMU samples, board identity, and firmware.

Time distribution

hostclock.* disciplines Linux clocks. ptpmanager.* manages PTP for LiDAR interfaces.

Operating system

Yocto builds the image and package. systemd mounts storage, launches Rover as root, restarts failures, and imposes a stop timeout.

What happens from boot to shutdown

  1. Yocto-built Linux boots. Storage and networking come up.
  2. systemd starts phoenix-rover.service. Its pre-run helper performs update/setup work, then /usr/bin/rover starts.
  3. main() creates one Rover. Because Rover derives from QCoreApplication, it owns the process event loop.
  4. The constructor wires the world. Settings, network services, microcontroller, timers, and startup gates are connected before recording starts.
  5. slotStart() creates the dataset and acquisition objects. Navigation and recorder managers begin operating through events and worker threads.
  6. GNSS establishes trustworthy time. Rover may correct the Linux system clock and RTC, then arm time-sensitive validation.
  7. A shutdown request fans out. Recorder managers flush and announce readiness. Rover waits, optionally powers sensors off, then quits the event loop.
  8. systemd observes the exit. With Restart=always, a normal stop may restart unless the requested post-shutdown action stops the service or powers off.
Do not begin by changing hardware state. First learn with logs, code tracing, unplugged test fixtures, or a designated development Rover. Confirm whether a unit is recording and whether stopping its service is acceptable.

Repository reading path

  1. rover/rover.h, then constructor, slotStart(), slotRequestShutDown(), slotTryShutdown(), and main() in rover.cpp.
  2. rover/CMakeLists.txt to see the Qt modules, dependencies, generated resources, and target.
  3. phoenix-rover.service and phoenix-rover.bb under buildimage-yocto/.../recipes-phoenixlidar/phoenix-rover/.
  4. nav.h/.cpp, hostclock.*, and ptpmanager.*.
  5. common/abstractconnection.*, then one small consumer such as rover/ioport.*.
  6. One sensor family end to end: its settings, controller, recorder, worker, parser, log output, and error path.

A realistic first week

  • Draw the startup and shutdown sequences from code without changing anything.
  • Build the Rover target and identify the executable, linked Qt version, and build commit.
  • On a development unit, collect systemctl status phoenix-rover, recent journal output, stable serial-device names, network interfaces, clocks, and PTP devices.
  • Trace one GNSS byte from the device connection into its parser and log file.
  • Trace one shutdown request until every recorder reports ready.
  • Write down unknown hardware terms and ask which Rover model is your primary development target.

Proven material to keep open

  • Qt: QCoreApplication explains the headless event loop at Rover's center.
  • Qt: Threads and QObjects is essential before touching worker lifetime or cross-thread calls.
  • systemd.service defines how systemd supervises Rover.
  • Yocto Project Quick Build supplies the vocabulary behind recipes, layers, machines, and images.
